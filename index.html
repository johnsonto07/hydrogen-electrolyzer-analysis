Hydrogen electrolysis, personal project, January to May 2026

How much of the current became hydrogen?

I built a graphite-electrode electrolysis cell, ran 18 timed trials from 4.0 to 6.5 V, and collected the hydrogen by water displacement. Faraday's law says how much hydrogen the charge passed should make. The best runs collected 88% of it.

The cell

A beaker, two graphite rods, a bench power supply, and a pair of inverted collection tubes.

Method

  • ElectrolyteBaking soda (NaHCO₃) in 500 mL water
  • Voltage4.0 to 6.5 V in 0.5 V steps

Getting it to work

My first runs in plain water made almost no gas. Pure water conducts poorly, so very little current flowed. Dissolving baking soda raised the conductivity enough to get steady bubbling, and every recorded trial uses that electrolyte. The pure-water runs were observed but not recorded.

What Faraday's law predicts

Every H₂ molecule at the cathode takes two electrons, so the charge passed sets the most hydrogen a run can make.

Cathode2H₂O + 2e⁻ → H₂ + 2OH⁻

Anode2H₂O → O₂ + 4H⁺ + 4e⁻

Overall2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂

Voltage only matters through current. A higher voltage pushes more current through the electrolyte, more current means more charge in 60 seconds, and more charge means more hydrogen. That is why current vs. hydrogen is the cleanest test of the theory.

VH₂=I · t2F·Vm

F = 96,485 C/mol. Vm = 24.0 L/mol, the molar volume of a gas near 20 °C and 1 atm. Faradaic efficiency is measured volume divided by this predicted volume.

Worked example: trial 12, 5.5 V

  1. Charge passed0.126 A × 60 s = 7.56 C
  2. Moles of electrons7.56 C ÷ 96,485 C/mol = 7.84 × 10⁻⁵ mol
  3. Moles of H₂ (2 e⁻ each)3.92 × 10⁻⁵ mol
  4. Predicted volume3.92 × 10⁻⁵ mol × 24,000 mL/mol = 0.940 mL
  5. Measured volume0.830 mL
  6. Faradaic efficiency0.830 ÷ 0.940 = 88.3%

This was the best single trial.

Results

Hydrogen tracked current closely. Efficiency climbed from 66% at 4.0 V to 88% at 5.5 V, then fell once gas started escaping the tubes.

Each extra amp made 90% of the predicted hydrogen

Across all 18 trials, hydrogen volume rises in a straight line with current (R² = 0.991). The fitted slope is 6.74 mL per amp over a 60 s run. Faraday's law predicts 7.46 mL per amp, so each additional unit of current produced about 90% of the theoretical hydrogen.

The fit crosses zero current at about −0.07 mL. That behaves like a fixed loss on every run, possibly hydrogen dissolving into the electrolyte or bubbles staying stuck to the electrode. A fixed loss is a bigger share of a small volume, which explains why the 4.0 V runs, at about 0.28 mL, had the lowest efficiency.

Efficiency peaked at 5.5 V

Mean efficiency rose from 66.1% at 4.0 V to 87.7% at 5.5 V. At 6.0 V and above, bubbling got aggressive enough that some gas escaped past the collection tube (I noted this in the raw data for all six of those trials), and efficiency dropped to 82–85%.

So 5.5 V was the best operating point for this setup. Going higher made hydrogen faster but lost more of it to collection.

Nothing happens below about 3 V

Current rose linearly with voltage, about 0.057 A per volt (R² = 0.974 across all trials). Extending that line back to zero current gives about 3.1 V.

Splitting water only needs 1.23 V in theory. The extra ~1.9 V goes to overpotential at the graphite electrodes and to pushing current through a weak bicarbonate electrolyte. Those losses are why industrial electrolyzers use concentrated KOH and nickel or catalyst-coated electrodes.

Where the missing hydrogen went

Every trial collected less than Faraday's law predicts. These are the likely reasons.

  1. Gas escaping the tube. At 6.0 and 6.5 V, some bubbles visibly escaped past the collection tube. This explains the efficiency drop after 5.5 V.
  2. Fixed loss per run. The −0.07 mL intercept in the current fit points to a loss that doesn't scale with current, like dissolved gas or bubbles stuck to the rod. That is about 17% of the predicted hydrogen at 4.0 V but only 5% at 6.5 V.
  3. Electrode spacing. I set the spacing by hand, and it was hard to keep identical. Current visibly rose when the rods sat closer together. Current varied 5–16% between the three trials at each voltage.
  4. Reading small volumes. Most runs collected under 1 mL, so a small reading error is a large percentage.
  5. Current drift. Current can shift slightly during a run, and each trial has one recorded current value.

What I'd change next time

  • Switch to a stronger electrolyte like dilute KOH to lower resistance.
  • Fix the electrodes in a holder so the spacing is the same every run.
  • Run for 3–5 minutes instead of 60 s so each reading is several mL, not a fraction of one.
  • Use a wider or longer collection tube so high-voltage runs don't lose gas.
  • Log current every few seconds and integrate it for the charge, instead of using one reading.
